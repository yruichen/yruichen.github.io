Greater Bay Area Summer Camp: Across Cities and Industries

Reflections on student entrepreneurship, technology incubation, social innovation, and manufacturing across the Greater Bay Area.
July 19, 2026 · 6 min

In July 2026, I joined the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area Summer Camp as a student from Guangdong University of Technology. I began the trip with two straightforward hopes: to understand how innovation and entrepreneurship take shape in universities and companies, and to experience Hong Kong beyond the images I had seen of it. Moving between campuses, businesses, and city streets brought those two interests together more naturally than I had expected.

The welcome dinner in Hong Kong was a quiet beginning to the week. There was no single defining moment, only the slight formality of meeting one another for the first time and the gradual ease of conversation around the table. Looking back, I remember it as a brief pause at the start of the journey, when most of what we would later see and discuss was still ahead of us.

At The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, we were introduced to PolyU's innovation and entrepreneurship programme. What interested me most was the way the university turns entrepreneurship from an individual interest into a structured learning opportunity in which students can participate. It suggested that developing a project could be more than an extracurricular experiment; it could also become part of a sustained educational experience. I left with something concrete to work towards: if the opportunity arises, I hope to return to PolyU one day as a participant rather than a visitor.

Cyberport offered a different perspective. We encountered technology products developed within its incubation ecosystem, and the idea of "incubation" became much less abstract when viewed through actual products. A technical concept must eventually meet real users, practical constraints, and a clear purpose before it can become something useful. I found this product-centred perspective especially valuable because it brought every discussion of innovation back to a simple question: what problem does the technology actually solve?

Outside the scheduled visits, I finally had time to explore Central on foot. The district is remarkably compact, with towers, slopes, footbridges, and narrow streets layered closely together. Glass facades reflect older buildings and shop signs, and the character of a street can change within a few minutes' walk. My first impression of Hong Kong was therefore not simply one of prosperity, but of an urban density that remains surprisingly legible. Traces of different periods sit side by side, each retaining something of its own rhythm and everyday life.

A ride through the city at sunset remains one of my clearest memories. As we moved forward, sunlight appeared and disappeared between the buildings, gradually warming the view outside the window. The pace of the day's visits briefly softened. There was no need to interpret the scene or hurry towards another destination; we simply followed the city and watched the light recede between the towers.

As the sky turned blue, lights began to take over the streets and the water. It was a short interval between day and night, when the city's sharp outlines seemed gentler. The camera recorded a night view, but what I remember more clearly are the minutes before I pressed the shutter: people still moving, distant lights appearing one by one, and the surroundings becoming quieter than they had been during the day.

Victoria Harbour opened the city up again. Light along both shores, the sea breeze, and the skyline stretching across the water formed the Hong Kong I had long known through photographs. Standing there in person, however, no single landmark mattered as much as the atmosphere created by all three. During the day, I had encountered Hong Kong through its universities and innovation ecosystem. By the harbour at night, the city felt more spacious, unhurried, and worth pausing in for a little longer.

A visit to a Hong Kong social organisation supporting local farmers revealed another side of the city. We followed the staff into the fields and experienced part of the farmers' daily work. The urban landscape was never far away, yet time seemed to move differently on the farm. Supporting agriculture was not presented as a broad charitable slogan, but as sustained attention to production, distribution, and the circumstances of farmers themselves. Walking through the fields made social innovation feel less dependent on visible technology. It can begin just as meaningfully with land, livelihoods, and particular communities.

The AI workshop had the more focused pace of a small engineering exercise. We worked with embedded-AI robots and completed one as a team. The assembly and debugging were relatively modest tasks, but they were enough to bring classroom concepts into our hands. Software, hardware, and physical behaviour had to align, and even a minor issue became immediately visible in the result. Dividing the work, discussing problems together, and finally seeing the robot operate gave the exercise a sense of completion that a demonstration alone could not have provided.

The company visits later in the programme expanded the industrial scale of our discussions. Haid Group works across modern agriculture and animal husbandry, with activities spanning feed, breeding, animal healthcare, intelligent farming, and food processing. In this setting, technology was not a label attached to agriculture from the outside; it was embedded in the everyday processes of breeding, farming, and production. I had previously associated innovation more readily with digital products and intelligent devices. Haid offered a useful reminder that established industries also contain complex and enduring technological questions.

At Yikang, we gained hands-on exposure to the processing of medical equipment. Because these products are intended for real clinical use, precision and standardisation are closely tied to their eventual reliability. The visit highlighted a quieter side of engineering: dependable equipment rarely rests on one dramatic breakthrough, but on many careful, repeatable, and well-controlled steps.

The automobile production line at GAC Aion presented manufacturing at a much larger scale. Walking alongside the line, we could see separate processes connect continuously as components gradually became complete vehicles. A showroom naturally directs attention towards design and performance. The production line offered a different view, revealing the manufacturing capability behind the finished product through automation, production rhythm, and coordination between people and machines.

After the company visits, we returned to Guangdong University of Technology and took this group photograph outside the Engineering Training Center. It is an informal image, but it preserves an important part of the experience: people from different backgrounds meeting through the programme and becoming familiar with one another through consecutive days of visits, practical activities, and conversation. Rather than forcing the journey into a neat conclusion, I prefer to think of it as an experience whose influence will continue to unfold.

Looking through these photographs, many once-abstract terms now have places and experiences attached to them. Innovation and entrepreneurship can take the form of a student programme at PolyU, a growing technology product at Cyberport, an organisation working in the fields, or the disciplined processes behind medical equipment and automobile manufacturing. These settings are very different, but each asks ideas to withstand the conditions of the real world. For me, the most lasting outcome of the summer camp was a clearer sense of where I would like to go next, together with a stronger willingness to seek out the next opportunity and take part in it fully.
